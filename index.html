What the paper proves

In the 100-number game, both uniform random guessing and midpoint play have their highest explosion probability at move 7, but the two distributions are fundamentally different. Random play spreads mass over many later rounds; midpoint play compresses almost all mass into the first seven moves.

Core conceptual distinction

A player's “best move” depends on the objective. If you only care about surviving this turn, all feasible guesses are equivalent. If you care about shrinking the interval as fast as possible, the median is uniquely optimal. If you care about maximizing your own chance of surviving the entire game, the problem becomes a dynamic program.

1. Explosion-time distributions

Switch between the exact 100-number distributions. The shared peak at move 7 hides a large structural difference: random play has a long right tail, while midpoint play packs most mass into the earliest feasible depths.

Random guessing

Exact recursion yields a spread-out distribution with expected explosion time about 7.48 moves. The mode is move 7, but only at about 14.81%.

Midpoint policy

The game becomes a deterministic search tree. For 100 numbers, the move probabilities are 1%, 2%, 4%, 8%, 16%, 32%, 37% on moves 1 through 7.

Why the same peak?

Because 100 lies between 2^6 = 64 and 2^7 = 128. In both models, the natural scale is logarithmic, so the modal move sits near depth 7.

2. Exact recursion

The paper's random-policy analysis is built from a finite-state transition kernel on the current feasible-set size.

Current feasible size = s

Explosion on this move:
p(explode | s) = 1 / s

Nonterminal transition to size u (1 ≤ u ≤ s − 1):
p(s → u) = 2u / s²

Explosion-time recursion:
p_s(1) = 1 / s
p_s(t) = Σ[u=1 to s−1] (2u / s²) · p_u(t−1), t ≥ 2
1
Local hazard

With s feasible numbers and a uniformly random guess, the current player hits the bomb with exact probability 1/s.

2
State reduction

If the guess misses, the interval shrinks to one side. The probability of landing in a new state of size u is exactly 2u/s².

3
Finite-state recursion

Because the next state depends only on the current size, explosion times and player risks can be computed exactly by dynamic recursion.

3. Which player is most likely to explode?

For random play, once the move distribution p_n(t) is known, the explosion probability of player j among m cyclic players is the sum over moves j, j+m, j+2m, ...

P(player j explodes) = Σ[ℓ ≥ 0] p_n(j + ℓm)

4. Three decision objectives

The paper separates three different notions of “best move.” This distinction is the conceptual heart of the analysis.

Objective What it optimizes Best action
Immediate survival Probability of not exploding on the current move All feasible guesses are equivalent
Social shrinking Expected size of the next feasible interval The median guess is optimal
Personal long-run survival Probability that I am not the eventual victim Requires dynamic programming
Immediate survival is action-invariant.

If the current interval has size s, every legal guess hits the bomb with probability 1/s. No guess improves your one-step chance of survival.

Median play is socially efficient.

Choosing the median minimizes the expected size of the next interval, which explains why binary-search play compresses explosion times into early moves.

Self-preservation is dynamic.

Once players care about who eventually explodes, the game ceases to be myopic. The optimal action depends on the future turn structure and must be solved by a Bellman recursion.

5. Bellman framework

For a focal player, the exact self-preservation problem can be written as a finite-state dynamic program.

V(s, r) = max over guesses k in {1,...,s} of

((k−1)/s) · V(k−1, r−1 mod m) + ((s−k)/s) · V(s−k, r−1 mod m)

where V(s, r) = maximal probability that the focal player ultimately survives, given interval size s and turn offset r until that player next acts.
State variable

The game state is not just the interval size s. One also needs the cyclic turn position among m players.

Action variable

The choice of pivot k changes the two possible continuation states and therefore changes the focal player's future risk profile.

Main takeaway

A strategy that shrinks the interval fastest need not be the strategy that maximizes one particular player's eventual survival probability.

6. What this generalizes to

The paper begins with the familiar 100-number game, but all exact recursions extend to arbitrary n and arbitrary numbers of cyclic players m.

General n

The random-policy recursion depends only on the current feasible-set size. This makes the model scale naturally from the 100-number game to any ordered set {1,...,n}.

p_n(t) = exact explosion probability on move t

E_n = expected explosion move

P_{n,m}(j) = probability that player j explodes among m cyclic players

General m

Once the move distribution is known, player-wise explosion risk is obtained by summing over move indices congruent to each player modulo m.

Player j acts on moves:
j, j+m, j+2m, ...

Hence
P_{n,m}(j) = p_n(j) + p_n(j+m) + p_n(j+2m) + ...
